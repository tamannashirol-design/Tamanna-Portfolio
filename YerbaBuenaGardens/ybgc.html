The problem

The Gardens aren’t boring. They’re invisible to Gen Z.

They have no digital footprint in the one place this age group actually looks. I interviewed 5 staff and leaders, then 6 people aged 18–25: 4 who had never visited and 2 who had.

“That’s the one group we’ve never figured out the magic pill for, at seven different parks and recreation departments, seven different cities.”

Executive Director, YBG

“I don’t think people are going on our website to look things up. People go on social media to look things up.”

Special Projects Manager, YBG

An awareness gap

People don’t know the Gardens exist, or what they offer.

The wrong channels

Gen Z finds events on social media which the Gardens don't use.

The flow

Everything in the app leads back to the Gardens.

v1 to v2

The first version worked but it didn’t look like the Gardens.

The existing system wasn’t expressing the place, so I replaced it with something calmer, closer to how the Gardens feel: an escape from the city’s noise.

Cleaner typefaces

Changed to a complimentary font pairing which feel similar.

Hierarchy fixed

One clear thing per screen, so the eye knows where to go first.

Color tightened

A calm base with one small accent for the moments that matter.

Events

Three versions of Events. One made it.

Failed · Version 1

The first build

The Events page from the original Lovable build, which I tested in two rounds. It didn’t hold up.

  • Too small to read comfortably.
  • Poor craft.
  • The old design system.
  • Bad hierarchy.
Failed · Version 2

The booklet flip

Flipping through events like pages looked charming, but it didn’t match how people think about an events list. They wanted to scan, not turn pages.

Chosen · Version 3

The poster scroll

Events as posters you scroll through, with a List and Calendar toggle for people who plan ahead. It keeps the Gardens’ own posters front and center and lets people scan fast.

Events · RSVP

The ticket tear

When you RSVP, your ticket tears off the poster. A small moment that makes the commitment feel real.

Badges

Testing showed the badge flow was confusing, so I rebuilt it as collectible cards.

v1 · What testing found

The weakest task, at 53.3%

In v1 testing, earning a badge had the lowest success of the five tasks, with the most errors and the longest time. People couldn’t tell how a badge was earned or what it was for.

Cards you want to collect

Each badge opens into a card with tilt and a foil sweep, collected like a set.

Rarity you can feel

Rare and epic cards are metallic, with a light that catches as you move.

Rewards on your profile

Rewards now show on the profile, with the badges you need to collect to unlock each one.

Missed badges

A section for what you could have earned, so the system tells you what to do next.

Decisions
What I cut

Replaced with “Open in Maps”

I designed a full map first. A good one takes a bigger team, so the app opens Apple Maps or Google Maps instead.

What I borrowed

Feed restyled only

It’s designed to work like the short-video feeds people already know, so there was nothing to reinvent. The effort went into Events and Badges.

What I left for later

Payments paused

I started building them in Cursor and hit my credit limit. The credits I had left went to Events and Badges.

Built with AI

Designed in Figma, built in Lovable, refined with Claude Code.

I designed the screens in Figma, generated the first build in Lovable, then iterated with Claude Code.

A Figma file built to be handed off.

Handing a file to Lovable and Cursor is unforgiving, since nothing carries over automatically. So I built text and color styles and auto layout into every screen, so it could be built as a system.

One prompt, one result

A real prompt I wrote for the badges page, with the order, sizes and spacing spelled out, next to the screen it produced.

A bug Figma would never show

A Figma prototype only plays the paths I drew. Using the real app, with real state and real taps, uncovered usability issues that two rounds of testing on the prototype never did.

Reflection

What this taught me.

01

Small prompts beat one big one

Describing the whole app at once burned tokens fast. I only got usable results once I broke each feature into small prompts in a fixed sequence. It also made me define each screen's behavior before building it, which is the same discipline a handoff needs.

02

The MVP was for testing, not launch

I built it to show the idea works and to test it with real people. Making it secure and public is the IT team’s job, and knowing where my part ends is part of the work.

03

My biggest design decision wasn't the strongest lever

Badges got the most design effort and were the hardest feature to use in testing. When I asked what would bring people back, events beat rewards. Next time I’ll test the core idea earlier, not just how usable it is.
